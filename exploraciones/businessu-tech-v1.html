About the project

Context, problem and objective

CONTEXT

Business U is an e-learning platform that evolved without a unified design system, resulting in fragmented user experiences and inefficient design workflows.

PROBLEM

As the platform grew, multiple teams designed independently without a shared system, leading to inconsistent experiences across products.

OBJECTIVE

Create a cohesive, scalable design system to improve consistency, collaboration, and speed across a growing multidisciplinary team in four months.

Step 01 / 05Aug – Sep 2025
01

Foundations

Before any component could be designed, the team needed a shared language. The Foundations stage was about understanding the current state of design across the platform, making strategic decisions about how the system should work, and building the structural backbone that everything else would depend on.

This wasn't just an audit. It was the deliberate act of turning design chaos into a scalable, documented, and collaborative system.

Discovery & analysis

Mapping the existing landscape

01 · AUDIT

Mapping current practices

The first step was a thorough audit of all active Figma files across teams. The goal was to identify recurring components, inconsistencies, and design patterns that had emerged organically without a system behind them.

02 · BENCHMARK

Benchmarking design systems

In parallel, a competitive benchmarking exercise was conducted, studying how mature design systems such as Material Design, Polaris, and Carbon approach documentation, tokenization, and component architecture.

03 · PLAN

Action plan creation

These two inputs, the internal audit and external references, formed the foundation of a clear, evidence-based action plan.

Strategy & roadmap

From findings to a structured plan

With the audit and benchmarking complete, the insights were translated into a concrete roadmap spanning four months. Each phase was scoped to deliver tangible outputs, from style decisions and token structures to fully documented components.

The roadmap served not just as a project plan, but as a communication tool. It aligned stakeholders, set clear expectations across teams, and established the design system as a strategic initiative rather than a side project.

Alignment process

Figma file architecture

A critical early decision was how to structure the Figma workspace itself. Access levels were defined to ensure the right people could contribute, review, or consume the system without breaking it.

  • Structured the file into five sections: Introduction, How It Works, Brand UI Kit, Style Guide and Tokenization, and Components
  • The Introduction covers the system's purpose and guiding principles
  • The How It Works section onboards new contributors to the system
  • The Brand UI Kit consolidates all visual identity assets in one place
  • The Style Guide and Tokenization layer establishes the visual language and design tokens
  • The Components section houses the full documented library
Standard for every component

Component documentation framework

Each component page follows a structured template: a Header with naming conventions and usage context, a Properties section detailing variants, states, and behaviors, a Specifications section covering spacing, sizing, correct / incorrect uses, accessibility considerations, and a Component Set with all visual variants organized in Figma. This framework eliminated ambiguity for developers and designers alike.

AnnotationAnyone opening the file could immediately understand how a component works, when to use it, and how to implement it.

T.01

Primitive tokens

Established a primitive token layer first, defining raw values for color, typography, spacing, border radius, and sizing, creating a single source of truth before any semantic decisions were made.

T.02

Semantic tokens

Built semantic tokens on top of primitives, mapping values to roles and contexts rather than specific styles, enabling two complete product themes: one for Business U and one for Mimic App, a separate brand initiative, eliminating redundant work across both products.

T.03

Two dimensions

Structured tokens across two dimension axes: product theme (Business U vs Mimic App) and display mode (light and dark), resulting in four token configurations maintained from a single organized foundation.

T.04

Token structure

Organized the token structure following a clear naming convention: category, property, variant, and state, making tokens immediately legible for both designers working in Figma and developers consuming them in code.

Typography

Why Mulish?

Mulish
RegularSemiboldExtrabold
Aa Bb Cc Dd Ee Ff Gg Hh 0123456789

Familiarity was a valid input, but the final decision was anchored in function.

Mulish is a geometric sans-serif with a clean, open structure that performs well at both display and body sizes, making it versatile across a learning platform where hierarchy and readability are critical. Its high x-height improves legibility on screen, particularly at smaller sizes common in UI contexts. The rounded letterforms reduce cognitive load during extended reading sessions, which matters significantly in an e-learning environment where users spend long periods consuming content.

Media queries

Grids & breakpoints

The decision to commit to exactly three breakpoints was deliberate. Adding more would have created maintenance overhead without a proportional gain in user experience, while fewer would have left significant layout gaps for a platform used across very different screen sizes.

Desktop was prioritized as the primary experience given the nature of e-learning content, but tablet and mobile were treated as first-class targets rather than afterthoughts, each with its own column grid, gutter definitions, and margin specifications.

Elevations & blurs

Bringing order to a brand that loved effects

E.01

Documentation process

The brand had a strong tendency toward visual effects, so rather than restricting them, all elevation levels and blur values were formally catalogued and documented in the style guide, giving the team a defined set to pull from instead of improvising on every screen.

E.02

Definition of styles

Each elevation and blur definition was tested against accessibility standards, verifying sufficient contrast ratios and ensuring that layered effects never compromised the legibility of content sitting above or below them.

UX and product perspective

Style guide as a product decision, not just a design deliverable

Every element in the style guide was treated as a functional decision with a user on the other end of it, and that framing consistently produced more durable, intentional outcomes than a purely visual approach would have.

Token architecture

P.02

Typography

Components library

With the foundations and style guide in place, the work shifted to building the component library itself. This stage was about making deliberate decisions: what to build, in what order, and why.

Every component was scoped, tested, and documented before being marked complete, resulting in a library built on evidence rather than assumption.

Component inventory & mapping

Knowing what exists before building what's needed

I.01

Components inventory (Figma and Adobe XD)

All existing components were catalogued across both tools, revealing a fragmented landscape with duplicates, inconsistencies, and unused assets.

I.02

External design system review

Fluent, MUI, Fiori were benchmarked to set coverage expectations and identify what a mature library should include.

I.03

Gap and redundancy analysis

Cross-referencing internal inventory with external benchmarks surfaced what was missing and what existed in too many conflicting versions.

I.04

Component list for the roadmap

Every item on the final list was justified by a documented gap, a redundancy to resolve, or a confirmed product need. Nothing was built speculatively.

Design process

A process built around decisions, not just deliverables

STEP 01

Discovery and selection

Each component was evaluated for necessity, usage frequency, and cross-product applicability before any design work began.

STEP 02

Stakeholder and dev meetings

Regular syncs ensured components were technically feasible and aligned with business priorities from the start, reducing handoff friction.

STEP 03

Design and testing

Every component was designed, documented, and tested in Figma covering variants, states, responsive behavior, and brand alignment across both themes and modes.

STEP 04

Process repeated per round · feedback loops back to stakeholder and dev meetings

Accessibility

Accessibility as a design requirement, not an afterthought

Because the platform operates in the United States, legal compliance was a non-negotiable requirement rather than a best practice. An external accessibility expert was brought in to run a formal diagnostic, and the findings were used as the direct input for remediation. Each component was reviewed in coordination with the expert, and corrective actions were defined, prioritized, and tracked per component until the library met the required standard.

WCAG

WCAG 2.1 AA

Every component was tested against WCAG 2.1 AA standards, covering color contrast ratios, focus states, and touch target sizing across all variants and modes.

KEYBOARD

Keyboard navigation

Interactive components such as dropdowns, modals, and form elements were validated for keyboard navigation and screen reader compatibility, ensuring the system worked beyond the pointer and touch contexts.

CONTRAST

Contrast

Contrast was evaluated across both light and dark modes and both product themes, since passing in one combination does not guarantee compliance in another.

DOCS

Annotations

Accessibility annotations were included directly in each component's documentation, making compliance requirements visible and actionable for developers at the moment of implementation.

Round definition

Three rounds, atomic design, one direction

The library was delivered in three rounds following atomic design: atoms first, then molecules, then organisms. Atoms gave the team usable building blocks immediately while more complex components were still in progress.

Each round included Figma testing, functionality-accessibility validation, and an annotation pass covering design decisions, technical constraints, and marketing usage needs. Components were validated against both product themes and both display modes before being marked complete.

Step 04 / 05Per delivery round
04

Testing

Building the library was only half the work. The testing stage was designed to validate that the system performed as intended under real usage conditions, across different teams, roles, and contexts.

Rather than relying on internal review alone, the process brought in designers, developers, and an external accessibility expert to stress-test the library before it reached production. Findings were structured, prioritized, and fed back directly into the system.

Testing workshops

Putting the library in front of real users

W.01

Workshop setup and environment

Each component round was tested in structured workshops with 15 designers (remote and in-person). Sessions were organized per delivery round, using QA environments that closely mirrored production to ensure realistic validation beyond Figma.

W.02

Design system protocol

Participants completed scenario-based tasks using the library to assess consistency, usability, and brand alignment. Observations were logged in real time, followed by debriefs to identify patterns before synthesis.

W.03

Development testing and KPI definition

Validated components moved to dev testing, where feasibility, token usage, and responsiveness were evaluated. KPIs measured consistency, efficiency, and adoption across products.

W.04

Accessibility expert integration

An external expert conducted WCAG 2.1 AA diagnostics during testing. Issues were logged per component, prioritized by severity, and assigned to owners for structured remediation.

Feedback process

From raw insight to structured action

Insights collected during workshops were complemented by a dedicated Google Forms channel, giving participants an asynchronous way to submit observations, edge cases, and suggestions after sessions closed. Every submission was reviewed and classified into one of three categories.

  • Release 1 items entered the active design and development pipeline immediately, addressing issues that directly affected component usability or compliance.
  • Incremental items (release 2) were logged as planned enhancements to the design system, scoped for a future iteration once the core library reached stability.
  • Out of scope items were formally acknowledged and documented, but excluded from the current MVP due to deadline, time, and resource constraints.

This classification ensured that no feedback was lost, and that the team had a clear rationale for every decision made about what to act on and when.

Design and documentation improvements

Problems found, solutions applied

FIX.01

Inconsistent naming

Component names didn't align with token conventions, causing handoff confusion. The library was renamed using a standardized structure, and a naming guide was added.

FIX.02

Missing states

Key states (error, disabled, loading) were missing, leading to inconsistencies. A mandatory checklist was introduced, and gaps were retroactively filled.

FIX.03

Spacing definition gaps

Spacing was visually defined but not tied to tokens, creating ambiguity. Redlines were added, linking all spacing values to tokens.

FIX.04

Documentation clarity

Non-designers struggled with usage guidelines. Headers were simplified with plain-language summaries, and a quick-reference guide was created.

Step 05 / 05Nov 2025
05

Launch & training

Delivering the library was not the finish line. The launch stage was designed to ensure the system reached the team in a way that was structured, documented, and built for adoption. Final review sessions validated every critical item before publication.

A deliberate Figma governance plan defined how the system would be accessed and maintained. And a training program ensured that designers, developers, and adjacent teams knew how to use the system from day one.

Final review sessions

Nothing ships without a sign-off

CHECK 01

Validation

Validation sessions with developers, the accessibility consultant, and design colleagues produced a triaged list of critical issues, each assigned an owner and resolved before publication.

CHECK 02

Documentation

Component documentation was reviewed for completeness and structured as the single source of truth inside Figma, following the same principle used in platforms like Zeroheight or GitHub Pages where documentation lives alongside or adjacent to the system it describes.

CHECK 03

Go-live

A specific go-live date and time was defined, creating accountability across disciplines and marking a clear transition from build to active use.

Figma publication plan

A library is only as good as its governance

G.01

Review of Figma plan

The Figma plan was reviewed to confirm file organization, version history, and access structure supported the library before publishing. Publication priorities defined which components needed to be verified as complete before go-live.

G.02

KPIs

Usage KPIs were configured from day one using Figma analytics, tracking insertions, detach rates, and viewer activity to monitor adoption and flag components being bypassed after launch.

G.03

Access

Editor access was limited to the core design team. All other roles, including developers and marketing, were assigned viewer permissions to ensure the library could be referenced and inspected without risk of unintended modifications.

Learnings and challenges

What the process taught us

Prioritize research

A full audit before building prevents redundancy and gives every decision a documented foundation.

Atomic design

Atomic design requires strict sequencing. Deviating even slightly introduced inconsistencies that required rework at higher levels.

Convention naming

Token naming needs dev alignment from day one. What made sense in Figma did not always translate cleanly to code.

Governance is key

A living system needs an owner. Without a defined maintainer, design systems drift after launch.

Scope creep happens

Scope will always expand. Building flexibility into the process is not optional.

Documentation

Documentation is a product in itself. Teams struggled not with components, but with incomplete documentation.

Coordination

Remote and in-person coordination across a team of 15 compressed some feedback windows.

Accessibility considerations

Accessibility remediation introduced scope that delayed two components and required roadmap adjustments.
